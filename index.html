The Haberdasher’s Puzzle

Where it comes from

Henry Ernest Dudeney (1857–1930) was an English writer and one of the great puzzle inventors. He posed this problem in 1902 in his newspaper column in the Weekly Dispatch, and it appeared in his book The Canterbury Puzzles (1907), where it is told as a tale of a haberdasher among Chaucer’s pilgrims. The challenge: cut an equilateral triangle into as few pieces as possible that can be rearranged into a square. Dudeney’s answer uses four pieces, and no dissection with fewer pieces is known. He also built a hinged model, with the pieces joined in a chain, so the triangle can be folded into the square without taking anything apart.

Equal areas fix the size

If the square has side 1, the triangle must also have area 1. For a triangle with side s that means (√3/4)·s² = 1, so s = 2/31/4 ≈ 1.5197 and its height is 31/4 ≈ 1.3161.

How the cuts work

Call the triangle ABC with apex C, and let L and R be the midpoints of the sides AC and BC. Two of the pieces are swung through 180° about L and R. Because L and R are midpoints, A and B both land exactly on C. The three 60° corners at A, B and C then add up to 180°, which makes a straight edge: the top side of the square.

The remaining freedom is where to place P on the base. Cut along the line from P to R. The distance from L to that line is then half the square’s side exactly when |PR| = 1. That single condition fixes P, and the other cut points follow.

The bigger picture

The Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem (early 1800s) says any two polygons of equal area can be cut into a finite number of pieces that rearrange into each other. Dudeney’s puzzle is a famously economical example. In three dimensions this fails, which was settled by Max Dehn in 1900 and is the answer to Hilbert’s third problem. Hinged dissections like Dudeney’s have been studied too: in 2012, Abbott, Abel, Charlton, Demaine, Demaine and Kominers proved that any two polygons of equal area have a hinged dissection.
